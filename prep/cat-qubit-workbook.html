The one-paragraph version

Store a qubit in a microwave resonator as two coherent states, \(|0\rangle\approx|{+\alpha}\rangle\) and \(|1\rangle\approx|{-\alpha}\rangle\). Engineer a loss channel that removes photons in pairs, \(L_2=\sqrt{\kappa_2}(a^2-\alpha^2)\), so these two states are the only places the resonator can rest. A bit flip now means crossing phase space against that restoring flow, so it is exponentially rare in \(|\alpha|^2\). A phase flip is a single lost photon, which only becomes more likely in proportion to \(|\alpha|^2\). You are left with a qubit that has essentially one kind of error, and a repetition code can fix that.

Before anything: the notation

How to read quantum symbols out loud

Most of the scary part is notation. Here is every symbol this workbook uses, with how you would say it.

\(|\psi\rangle\)
"ket psi". A quantum state, think column vector. \(\langle\psi|\) ("bra psi") is its conjugate transpose, a row vector.
\(\langle\phi|\psi\rangle\)
"the overlap of phi and psi". An inner product, a single number. Its squared magnitude is the probability of finding \(\psi\) in \(\phi\).
\(|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|\)
"the projector onto psi". Column times row gives a matrix.
\(a,\ a^\dagger\)
"a" and "a dagger". \(a\) removes one photon, \(a^\dagger\) adds one. The dagger \(^\dagger\) means conjugate transpose.
\(n=a^\dagger a\)
"the number operator". It measures how many photons there are.
\(|n\rangle\)
"Fock state n". Exactly \(n\) photons. \(|0\rangle\) is the vacuum.
\(H\)
"the Hamiltonian". The energy operator. It sets how the state moves when nothing is lost.
\(\rho\)
"rho", the density matrix. A state that may also include classical uncertainty.
\(\dot\rho\)
"rho dot". The time derivative \(d\rho/dt\).
\([A,B]\)
"the commutator of A and B", which is \(AB-BA\). Zero means the order does not matter.
\(\{A,B\}\)
"the anticommutator", which is \(AB+BA\).
\(\langle O\rangle\)
"the expectation value of O". The average you would measure, \(\mathrm{Tr}(O\rho)\).
\(\mathrm{Tr}\)
"trace". Sum of the diagonal of a matrix.
\(\kappa,\ \kappa_1,\ \kappa_2\)
"kappa". A rate, in events per unit time. \(1/\kappa\) is the typical waiting time.
\(\alpha\)
"alpha". A complex number: where the coherent state sits in phase space. \(|\alpha|^2\) is its average photon count.
\(\hbar=1\)
Units chosen so Planck's constant disappears. Energies and frequencies are the same thing.
Notebook 1 · Tutorial_OpenQuantumSystems

Density matrices and the Lindblad equation

When your system (the resonator) is coupled to something you do not track (cables, the fridge, a lossy buffer mode), its state is a density matrix \(\rho=\sum_i p_i|\psi_i\rangle\langle\psi_i|\). Purity \(\mathrm{Tr}\,\rho^2\) is 1 for a pure state and below 1 for a mixture.

Read it aloud"Rho equals the sum over i of p-i times the projector onto psi-i." The system is in state \(\psi_1\) with probability \(p_1\), in \(\psi_2\) with probability \(p_2\), and so on.
Like I'm fiveA ket is "I know exactly what the coin is doing." A density matrix is "I have a bag of coins, and some are doing one thing and some another." Losing photons into the environment is like having someone secretly take coins out. You no longer know exactly which state you have, so you need the bag description.

With a memoryless (Markovian) environment, \(\rho\) obeys

\[\dot\rho=-i[H,\rho]+\sum_k \mathcal D[L_k]\rho,\qquad \mathcal D[L]\rho=L\rho L^\dagger-\tfrac12\{L^\dagger L,\rho\}.\]
Read it aloud"Rho dot equals minus i times the commutator of H with rho, plus the sum over k of the dissipator of L-k acting on rho." And: "the dissipator of L on rho is L rho L-dagger, minus one half of the anticommutator of L-dagger-L with rho."
Term by term: \(-i[H,\rho]\) is the ordinary quantum motion (Schrödinger), the same as for a closed system. \(L\rho L^\dagger\) is "a jump happened": apply \(L\) (for example, remove a photon). \(-\tfrac12\{L^\dagger L,\rho\}\) is "no jump happened, but I update my belief". Every moment without a click makes states that would have jumped a bit less likely. It also keeps total probability equal to 1.
Like I'm fiveImagine a bucket of water sloshing back and forth. The first term is the sloshing. Each \(L\) is a hole in the bucket. \(L\rho L^\dagger\) is a drop falling out. The \(-\tfrac12\) term is the bookkeeping that says "if no drop fell, the bucket was probably less full than I thought." Pick different holes and you get different physics. Alice & Bob drill a very special hole, \(L_2\), that only lets out photons in pairs.

Each jump operator already includes the square root of its rate: single-photon loss at rate \(\kappa\) is \(L=\sqrt\kappa\,a\). That is why the code says jnp.sqrt(kappa) * a.

The workhorse identity for everything below: for any operator \(O\),

\[\frac{d\langle O\rangle}{dt}=i\langle[H,O]\rangle+\sum_k\Big\langle L_k^\dagger O L_k-\tfrac12\{L_k^\dagger L_k,O\}\Big\rangle .\]
Read it aloud"The rate of change of the average of O equals i times the average of the commutator of H with O, plus the sum over k of the average of L-k-dagger O L-k minus half the anticommutator of L-k-dagger-L-k with O."
Like I'm fiveThis is the same equation, rearranged to answer "how does one measurable number change?" instead of "how does the whole state change?" You will use it to get simple equations such as "photon number decays at rate κ" without solving for the whole matrix. Recipe: plug in \(H\), \(L\) and \(O\), then shuffle the operators using the commutator rules below until only \(O\)-like things remain.

Useful commutators: \([a,a^\dagger]=1\), \([a^\dagger a,a]=-a\), \([a,a^{\dagger2}]=2a^\dagger\), and \(a\,f(a^\dagger a)=f(a^\dagger a+1)\,a\).

Notebook erratum (cell 5). The dephasing rate \(\kappa_\varphi\) is described as a "single photon loss rate". It is the dephasing rate: \(L=\sqrt{\kappa_\varphi}\,a^\dagger a\) leaves photon number alone and scrambles phase.

1.1 · Pure or mixed?

Compute \(\mathrm{Tr}\,\rho^2\) for \(\rho_A=\tfrac12(|0\rangle\langle0|+|1\rangle\langle1|)\) and for \(\rho_B=|{+}\rangle\langle{+}|\). Both give 50/50 outcomes in the Z basis. What experiment tells them apart?
Write \(\rho_B\) as a 2×2 matrix. Its off-diagonal elements are the coherences.
Step 1: basis vectors. \(|0\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}\), \(|1\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}\). A bra is the conjugate transpose: \(\langle0|=(1\;\;0)\), \(\langle1|=(0\;\;1)\).
Step 2: projectors (column × row). \(|0\rangle\langle0|=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}(1\;\;0)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}\), \(|1\rangle\langle1|=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\).
Step 3: \(\rho_A\). \(\rho_A=\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\). Then \(\rho_A^2=\tfrac14\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\), so \(\mathrm{Tr}\rho_A^2=\tfrac14+\tfrac14=\tfrac12\). Mixed.
Step 4: \(\rho_B\). \(|+\rangle=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}\), so \(\rho_B=|+\rangle\langle+|=\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}(1\;\;1)=\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}\).
Step 5: square it. \(\rho_B^2=\tfrac14\begin{pmatrix}1\cdot1+1\cdot1&1\cdot1+1\cdot1\\1\cdot1+1\cdot1&1\cdot1+1\cdot1\end{pmatrix}=\tfrac14\begin{pmatrix}2&2\\2&2\end{pmatrix}=\rho_B\), so \(\mathrm{Tr}\rho_B^2=\tfrac12+\tfrac12=1\). Pure.
Step 6: Z basis looks the same. \(P(0)=\langle0|\rho|0\rangle\) is the top-left entry, which is \(\tfrac12\) for both.
Step 7: X basis tells them apart. \(P(+)=\langle+|\rho|+\rangle\). For \(\rho_A\): \(\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1\;\;1)\cdot\tfrac12 I\cdot\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}=\tfrac14\cdot2=\tfrac12\). For \(\rho_B\): \(\tfrac12(1\;\;1)\cdot\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}2\\2\end{pmatrix}=\tfrac14\cdot4=1\). The difference lives entirely in the off-diagonal entries (the coherences). Measure in the X basis: \(\rho_B\) always gives \(+\), \(\rho_A\) gives 50/50. For cats, this is exactly the difference between a real cat and a "dead or alive" mixture, and you will see it as Wigner fringes.

1.2 · Photon loss empties the cavity at rate κ

Take \(H=\omega a^\dagger a\) and \(L=\sqrt\kappa\,a\). Use the identity above to derive \(d\langle n\rangle/dt\) for \(n=a^\dagger a\), and solve it.
\([H,n]=0\). For the dissipator you need \(a^\dagger n a=a^\dagger a^\dagger a a=n^2-n\).
Step 1. Write down what you need: \(\frac{d\langle n\rangle}{dt}=i\langle[H,n]\rangle+\kappa\big\langle a^\dagger n a-\tfrac12(a^\dagger a\,n+n\,a^\dagger a)\big\rangle\).
Step 2. Build the matrices from scratch.
  2a. Pick a basis. Keep only 0, 1 or 2 photons, so \(|0\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}\), \(|1\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\0\end{pmatrix}\), \(|2\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\1\end{pmatrix}\). That is the "cutoff at 3 levels". The real space is infinite, and we cut it off so it fits in a matrix. In code this is dq.destroy(3).
  2b. The rule for \(a\). \(a|n\rangle=\sqrt n\,|n-1\rangle\): remove one photon and multiply by \(\sqrt n\). So \(a|0\rangle=0\) (nothing to remove), \(a|1\rangle=\sqrt1\,|0\rangle\), \(a|2\rangle=\sqrt2\,|1\rangle\).
  2c. Turn the rule into a matrix. Column \(n\) of the matrix is \(a|n\rangle\) written as a vector: column 0 \(=(0,0,0)^T\), column 1 \(=(1,0,0)^T\), column 2 \(=(0,\sqrt2,0)^T\). Side by side: \(a=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\0&0&\sqrt2\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix}\). The \(\sqrt1,\sqrt2,\dots\) always sit just above the diagonal.
  2d. The others follow. \(a^\dagger\) is the transpose, \(\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\1&0&0\\0&\sqrt2&0\end{pmatrix}\), with \(\sqrt n\) just below the diagonal, so \(a^\dagger|n\rangle=\sqrt{n+1}\,|n+1\rangle\). Multiply them: \(n=a^\dagger a=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\0&1&0\\0&0&2\end{pmatrix}\), which just counts photons.
  2e. Check. \(a^\dagger a|2\rangle=a^\dagger(\sqrt2|1\rangle)=\sqrt2\cdot\sqrt2\,|2\rangle=2|2\rangle\). Two photons, as expected.
Step 3. Hamiltonian term: \(H=\omega n\) and \([n,n]=nn-nn=0\). Diagonal matrices commute. This term contributes 0.
Step 4. First dissipator piece: \(a^\dagger n a=a^\dagger(a^\dagger a)a\). Use \(aa^\dagger=a^\dagger a+1\), so \(a^\dagger a=aa^\dagger-1\): \(a^\dagger(aa^\dagger-1)a=(a^\dagger a)(a^\dagger a)-a^\dagger a=n^2-n\).
Step 5. Second piece: \(\tfrac12(n\cdot n+n\cdot n)=n^2\).
Step 6. Subtract: \(\kappa(n^2-n-n^2)=-\kappa n\). So \(\frac{d\langle n\rangle}{dt}=-\kappa\langle n\rangle\).
Step 7. Solve the ODE. Let \(y=\langle n\rangle\), so \(\frac{dy}{dt}=-\kappa y\). Separate variables: \(\frac{dy}{y}=-\kappa\,dt\). Integrate both sides: \(\ln y=-\kappa t+C\). Exponentiate: \(y=e^{C}e^{-\kappa t}\). At \(t=0\), \(y(0)=e^C=|\alpha|^2\) (the coherent state starts with \(|\alpha|^2\) photons). So \(\langle n\rangle(t)=|\alpha|^2e^{-\kappa t}\). Check: differentiating gives \(-\kappa|\alpha|^2e^{-\kappa t}=-\kappa y\), as required. After \(t=1/\kappa\), 37% of the photons remain.

1.3 · The spiral in the notebook GIF

Same \(H\) and \(L\). Derive \(\langle a\rangle(t)\) for an initial coherent state \(|\alpha\rangle\). Why does the amplitude decay at \(\kappa/2\) when the energy decays at \(\kappa\)?
\(i[H,a]=-i\omega a\). For the dissipator, \(a^\dagger a a-\tfrac12(a^\dagger a\,a+a\,a^\dagger a)\), and use \(a a^\dagger a=(a^\dagger a+1)a\).
Step 1. Hamiltonian term with \(O=a\): you need \([a^\dagger a,a]=a^\dagger aa-aa^\dagger a\). Replace \(aa^\dagger=a^\dagger a+1\) in the second product: \(a^\dagger aa-(a^\dagger a+1)a=-a\).
Step 2. So \(i\langle[\omega a^\dagger a,a]\rangle=i\omega(-\langle a\rangle)=-i\omega\langle a\rangle\).
Step 3. Dissipator: \(\kappa\big(a^\dagger a\,a-\tfrac12(a^\dagger a\,a+a\,a^\dagger a)\big)=\tfrac\kappa2(a^\dagger a\,a-a\,a^\dagger a)=\tfrac\kappa2[a^\dagger a,a]=-\tfrac\kappa2a\).
Step 4. Add them: \(\frac{d\langle a\rangle}{dt}=-\big(i\omega+\tfrac\kappa2\big)\langle a\rangle\).
Step 5. Solve: \(\langle a\rangle(t)=\alpha\,e^{-i\omega t}\,e^{-\kappa t/2}\).
Step 6. Read it: \(e^{-i\omega t}\) is rotation by angle \(-\omega t\) (clockwise). \(e^{-\kappa t/2}\) shrinks the radius. Together they make a spiral into 0.
Step 7. Energy: \(|\langle a\rangle|^2=|\alpha|^2e^{-\kappa t}\). Squaring doubles the exponent, which matches 1.2. Amplitude decays at \(\kappa/2\) and energy at \(\kappa\).

1.4 · What does dephasing do?

Now \(L=\sqrt{\kappa_\varphi}\,n\). What happens to \(\langle n\rangle\)? To \(\langle a\rangle\)? Predict what the Wigner function of a coherent state looks like after a while, then check with the simulator below (set κ to 0).
\(n\) commutes with \(L\). For \(a\), push every \(n\) to the right of \(a\) using \(n\,a=a(n-1)\).
Step 1. \(\langle n\rangle\): with \(L=\sqrt{\kappa_\varphi}n\), the dissipator gives \(\kappa_\varphi(n\,n\,n-\tfrac12(n^2n+nn^2))=\kappa_\varphi(n^3-n^3)=0\). Every matrix here is diagonal, so everything commutes. \(\langle n\rangle\) is constant.
Step 2. \(\langle a\rangle\): you need \(\kappa_\varphi\big(n\,a\,n-\tfrac12(n^2a+a\,n^2)\big)\).
Step 3. Rule to move \(n\) past \(a\): \(n\,a=a(n-1)\). Check on \(|k\rangle\): \(na|k\rangle=\sqrt k\,(k-1)|k-1\rangle\) and \(a(n-1)|k\rangle=(k-1)\sqrt k|k-1\rangle\). They match.
Step 4. Apply it: \(n a n=a(n-1)n\), \(n^2a=a(n-1)^2\), and \(a n^2\) stays as it is.
Step 5. Bracket: \(a\big[(n^2-n)-\tfrac12(n^2-2n+1+n^2)\big]=a\big[n^2-n-n^2+n-\tfrac12\big]=-\tfrac12a\).
Step 6. So \(\frac{d\langle a\rangle}{dt}=-\frac{\kappa_\varphi}2\langle a\rangle\). The amplitude shrinks while the energy stays fixed, so the blob smears around a ring.
Step 7. Matrix picture: for a single entry \(\rho_{mn}\), \(\mathcal D[n]\rho\) gives \(\kappa_\varphi\big(mn-\tfrac12(m^2+n^2)\big)\rho_{mn}=-\tfrac{\kappa_\varphi}{2}(m-n)^2\rho_{mn}\). The diagonal (\(m=n\)) is untouched. Off-diagonals decay faster the farther they are from the diagonal. Dephasing is "erase the coherences".

1.5 · The Liouvillian ★

The notebook says the Lindblad equation is linear in \(\rho\), so it is a matrix \(\mathcal L\) acting on vectorized \(\rho\). With a Fock cutoff of 15, how big is that matrix? What does eigenvalue 0 mean, and what does the smallest nonzero \(|\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda|\) tell you?
\(\rho\) has \(15^2\) entries.
Step 1. \(\rho\) is \(15\times15\), which is 225 numbers. Stack its columns into one long vector \(|\rho\rangle\!\rangle\) of length 225.
Step 2. The Lindblad equation is linear in \(\rho\), so it becomes \(\frac{d}{dt}|\rho\rangle\!\rangle=\mathcal L|\rho\rangle\!\rangle\) with \(\mathcal L\) a \(225\times225\) matrix.
Step 3. Like any linear ODE: \(|\rho(t)\rangle\!\rangle=\sum_k c_k e^{\lambda_k t}|r_k\rangle\!\rangle\), where \(\lambda_k\) are the eigenvalues of \(\mathcal L\).
Step 4. \(\lambda=0\): \(e^{0t}=1\), so that part never changes. It is the steady state.
Step 5. All other eigenvalues have \(\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda<0\) and die out. The one with the smallest \(|\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda|\) survives longest. \(1/|\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda|\) is the slowest timescale in the system.
Step 6. For a cat qubit, that slowest process is the bit flip. Computing one eigenvalue gives \(\Gamma_{\rm bit}\) without simulating until a flip happens.

Notebook 3 · Introduction_to_Cats, cells 3–6

Coherent states and cats

A coherent state is an eigenstate of the annihilation operator, \(a|\alpha\rangle=\alpha|\alpha\rangle\). In the Fock basis

\[|\alpha\rangle=e^{-|\alpha|^2/2}\sum_{n}\frac{\alpha^n}{\sqrt{n!}}|n\rangle,\]
Read it aloud"Ket alpha equals e to the minus half mod-alpha squared, times the sum over n of alpha to the n over root n factorial, times ket n." In words, a coherent state is a specific mix of every photon number, weighted so the counts are Poisson-distributed.
Like I'm fiveA coherent state is the most "classical" light there is, like a laser beam. It has a definite average brightness \(|\alpha|^2\) and a definite phase (the angle of \(\alpha\)). In phase space it is a single round blob, centered at the point \(\alpha\).

so photon number is Poisson with mean \(|\alpha|^2\). It is an ideal laser mode, and its Wigner function is a Gaussian blob at \(\alpha\). The cats are

\[|C_\pm\rangle=\mathcal N_\pm\big(|\alpha\rangle\pm|{-\alpha}\rangle\big),\qquad |0_L\rangle=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|C_+\rangle+|C_-\rangle)\approx|\alpha\rangle,\quad |1_L\rangle=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|C_+\rangle-|C_-\rangle)\approx|{-\alpha}\rangle .\]

Two-photon dissipation

Ordinary loss pulls everything to the vacuum. The trick is a loss channel that removes photons in pairs and is balanced by a two-photon drive:

\[L_2=\sqrt{\kappa_2}\,(a^2-\alpha^2),\qquad \dot\rho=\kappa_2\mathcal D[a^2-\alpha^2]\rho+\kappa_1\mathcal D[a]\rho .\]
Read it aloud"L-two equals root kappa-two times a-squared minus alpha-squared." "Rho dot equals kappa-two times the dissipator of a-squared minus alpha-squared, plus kappa-one times the dissipator of a." The first term is the engineered two-photon loss. The second is ordinary, unwanted single-photon loss.
Like I'm five\(a^2\) takes photons out two at a time, and the \(-\alpha^2\) part pumps them back in two at a time. The pumping and the loss cancel exactly when the light is at \(+\alpha\) or \(-\alpha\). Anywhere else the state gets pushed back toward one of those two points, like a marble rolling into one of two bowls. Pairs never change whether the photon count is even or odd, so the parity you start with is the parity you keep.

A state with \(L_2|\psi\rangle=0\) is "dark": the channel cannot touch it. Those dark states are where the system rests.

4.1 · The qubit is the dark subspace

Show \(L_2|{\pm\alpha}\rangle=0\). Why does that make every state in \(\mathrm{span}\{|\alpha\rangle,|{-\alpha}\rangle\}\), including mixtures, a steady state when \(\kappa_1=0\)?
\(a^2|{\pm\alpha}\rangle=(\pm\alpha)^2|{\pm\alpha}\rangle\).
Step 1. \(a^2|\alpha\rangle=a(a|\alpha\rangle)=a(\alpha|\alpha\rangle)=\alpha\,a|\alpha\rangle=\alpha^2|\alpha\rangle\).
Step 2. \(a^2|{-\alpha}\rangle=(-\alpha)^2|{-\alpha}\rangle=\alpha^2|{-\alpha}\rangle\). The minus sign squares away.
Step 3. So \((a^2-\alpha^2)|{\pm\alpha}\rangle=\alpha^2|{\pm\alpha}\rangle-\alpha^2|{\pm\alpha}\rangle=0\).
Step 4. Linearity: \((a^2-\alpha^2)(c_0|\alpha\rangle+c_1|{-\alpha}\rangle)=0\) for any \(c_0,c_1\). The whole 2D space is killed.
Step 5. For any \(\rho=\sum_ip_i|\psi_i\rangle\langle\psi_i|\) built from that space: \(L\rho=0\) and \(\rho L^\dagger=(L\rho)^\dagger=0\), so \(\mathcal D[L]\rho=L\rho L^\dagger-\tfrac12(L^\dagger L\rho+\rho L^\dagger L)=0-0-0=0\).
Step 6. With \(H=0\), \(\dot\rho=0\): every such state is a steady state. A whole qubit of them.

4.2 · Parity is conserved

Explain why vacuum flows to \(|C_+\rangle\) and \(|1\rangle\) flows to \(|C_-\rangle\), never the other way. Then run the simulator below for both starts.
\(a^2\) changes photon number by 2. What does it do to parity?
Step 1. Parity matrix: \(P=\mathrm{diag}(1,-1,1,-1,\dots)\).
Step 2. \(a^2\) maps \(|n\rangle\to\sqrt{n(n-1)}|n-2\rangle\). Its only nonzero entries sit two above the diagonal, linking even to even and odd to odd.
Step 3. Sandwich: \((PaP)_{n-1,n}=(-1)^{n-1}\sqrt n(-1)^n=-\sqrt n\), so \(PaP=-a\). Then \(Pa^2P=(PaP)(PaP)=(-a)(-a)=a^2\). Since \(P^2=1\), \(Pa^2=a^2P\), and \([P,L_2]=0\).
Step 4. Rate of change: \(\frac{d\langle P\rangle}{dt}=\kappa_2\langle L^\dagger PL-\tfrac12\{L^\dagger L,P\}\rangle\). Move \(P\) through \(L\): \(L^\dagger PL=PL^\dagger L\), so the bracket is \(PL^\dagger L-PL^\dagger L=0\). Parity is conserved.
Step 5. Vacuum \(|0\rangle\) has \(P=+1\). It must end in the even part of the steady space, which is \(|C_+\rangle\). \(|1\rangle\) has \(P=-1\) and ends at \(|C_-\rangle\).
Step 6. With single-photon loss: \(a\) anticommutes with \(P\), so \(a^\dagger Pa=-Pa^\dagger a\). That gives \(\frac{d\langle P\rangle}{dt}=\kappa_1\langle-Pn-Pn\rangle\approx-2\kappa_1\bar n\langle P\rangle\). Parity decays at \(2\kappa_1\bar n\), which is chapter 4.

Step 9. With \(\kappa_1\), real \(a\neq0\): divide by \(a\) to get \(-\kappa_2(a^2-\alpha^2)-\kappa_1/2=0\), so \(a^2=\alpha^2-\frac{\kappa_1}{2\kappa_2}\). The wells move slightly inward.

Where L₂ comes from: eliminating the buffer

Nature does not hand you \(a^2\) loss. Alice & Bob couple the memory \(a\) to a deliberately lossy buffer \(b\) so that two memory photons convert into one buffer photon, and they drive the buffer:

\[H=g_2a^{\dagger2}b+g_2^*a^2b^\dagger+\epsilon_d^*b+\epsilon_db^\dagger,\qquad L_b=\sqrt{\kappa_b}\,b .\]
Read it aloud"H equals g-two a-dagger-squared b, plus g-two-star a-squared b-dagger, plus epsilon-d-star b, plus epsilon-d b-dagger." Term 1: destroy one buffer photon, create two memory photons. Term 2: the reverse, two memory photons become one buffer photon. Terms 3 and 4: a drive that feeds the buffer. \(L_b\): the buffer leaks photons at rate \(\kappa_b\).
Like I'm fiveThe memory is a nice, quiet box. The buffer is a leaky box next to it. They are connected by a trade window: two photons from the memory swap for one in the buffer. The buffer leaks so fast that any photon arriving there is gone almost immediately, so pairs leave the memory permanently. That is your two-photon loss. Driving the buffer pushes pairs back in, and that sets the \(\alpha^2\).

If the buffer is very lossy, it follows the memory instantly, and you can eliminate it. Work each step on paper before you reveal it. This is the derivation judges are most likely to ask you to explain.

Step 1 · Equation of motion for b

Using \(\dot O=i[H,O]\) plus the damping from \(L_b\), write \(\dot b\).
\([b^\dagger,b]=-1\). The damping term is \(-\tfrac{\kappa_b}{2}b\), as in exercise 1.3.
Step 1. Heisenberg plus damping: \(\dot b=i[H,b]-\tfrac{\kappa_b}2b\). The damping term comes from 1.3 with \(a\to b\).
Step 2. Term by term, using \([b^\dagger,b]=-1\) and the fact that \(a\) commutes with \(b\): \([g_2a^{\dagger2}b,b]=0\), \([g_2^*a^2b^\dagger,b]=g_2^*a^2(-1)\), \([\epsilon_d^*b,b]=0\), \([\epsilon_db^\dagger,b]=-\epsilon_d\).
Step 3. So \(i[H,b]=-i(g_2^*a^2+\epsilon_d)\).
Step 4. Result: \(\dot b=-i(g_2^*a^2+\epsilon_d)-\tfrac{\kappa_b}2b\).

Step 2 · Adiabatic elimination

Assume \(\kappa_b\gg|g_2|,|\epsilon_d|\), so \(b\) relaxes much faster than \(a\) changes. Set \(\dot b\approx0\) and solve for \(b\).
One line of algebra.
Step 1. Fast buffer: \(b\) reaches its equilibrium almost instantly, so set \(\dot b=0\).
Step 2. \(0=-i(g_2^*a^2+\epsilon_d)-\tfrac{\kappa_b}2b\).
Step 3. Move \(b\) to one side: \(\tfrac{\kappa_b}2b=-i(g_2^*a^2+\epsilon_d)\).
Step 4. Multiply by \(2/\kappa_b\): \(b\approx-\tfrac{2i}{\kappa_b}(g_2^*a^2+\epsilon_d)\).

Step 3 · Equation of motion for a

Compute \(\dot a=i[H,a]\). Only one term of \(H\) contributes.
\([a^{\dagger2},a]=-2a^\dagger\).
Step 1. \(\dot a=i[H,a]\). There is no direct loss on \(a\) in this model.
Step 2. Only \(a^{\dagger2}\) fails to commute with \(a\). The \(a^2b^\dagger\) term and the drive terms commute with \(a\).
Step 3. \([a^{\dagger2},a]=a^\dagger[a^\dagger,a]+[a^\dagger,a]a^\dagger=-a^\dagger-a^\dagger=-2a^\dagger\).
Step 4. So \(\dot a=i\,g_2(-2a^\dagger)b=-2i\,g_2a^\dagger b\).

Step 4 · Substitute and match

Plug step 2 into step 3. Compare with the mean-field equation from 4.3, \(\dot a=-\kappa_2a^\dagger(a^2-\alpha^2)\), and read off \(\kappa_2\) and \(\alpha^2\).
\((-2i)(-2i)=-4\). Factor \(g_2^*\) out of the bracket.
Step 1. Substitute step 2 into step 3: \(\dot a=-2ig_2a^\dagger\cdot\big(-\tfrac{2i}{\kappa_b}\big)(g_2^*a^2+\epsilon_d)\).
Step 2. Constants: \((-2i)(-2i)=4i^2=-4\). So \(\dot a=-\tfrac{4g_2}{\kappa_b}a^\dagger(g_2^*a^2+\epsilon_d)\).
Step 3. Factor \(g_2^*\) out of the bracket: \(g_2\cdot g_2^*=|g_2|^2\), giving \(\dot a=-\tfrac{4|g_2|^2}{\kappa_b}a^\dagger\big(a^2+\tfrac{\epsilon_d}{g_2^*}\big)\).
Step 4. Target from 4.3: \(\dot a=-\kappa_2a^\dagger(a^2-\alpha^2)\).
Step 5. Match the prefactor: \(\kappa_2=\frac{4|g_2|^2}{\kappa_b}\). Match inside the bracket: \(-\alpha^2=\frac{\epsilon_d}{g_2^*}\), so \(\alpha^2=-\frac{\epsilon_d}{g_2^*}\).\[\boxed{\kappa_2=\frac{4|g_2|^2}{\kappa_b},\qquad \alpha^2=-\frac{\epsilon_d}{g_2^*}}\]

Step 5 · Make it intuitive

Why does \(\kappa_2\) grow like \(g_2^2\) but shrink as the buffer gets lossier? Connect it to something from photonics.
Think of the buffer as a broad bath with linewidth \(\kappa_b\). Fermi's golden rule.
Step 1. Fermi\'s golden rule: decay rate \(=2\pi|g|^2\times\)(density of final states at the transition energy).
Step 2. A buffer with linewidth \(\kappa_b\) has a Lorentzian density of states \(\rho(\omega)=\frac{\kappa_b/2\pi}{\omega^2+(\kappa_b/2)^2}\). On resonance, \(\rho(0)=\frac{2}{\pi\kappa_b}\).
Step 3. Rate: \(2\pi g_2^2\cdot\frac{2}{\pi\kappa_b}=\frac{4g_2^2}{\kappa_b}\). Same answer as the derivation.
Step 4. Intuition: a lossier buffer is a broader Lorentzian with a lower peak, so it is a worse "receiver" exactly on resonance. That is the Purcell effect: an emitter in a bad cavity decays at \(4g^2/\kappa\).
Step 5. When it fails: if \(\kappa_b\) is not the fastest rate, the buffer remembers things and you cannot set \(\dot b=0\).

Step 5. The effective coupling grows with photon number: \(g_2|\alpha|^2=4\), compared with \(\kappa_b=10\). Not \(\gg\).
Step 6. Verdict: expect visible disagreement between the two-mode and one-mode models. Sweep \(\kappa_b/g_2\) and plot where they diverge. That is a strong slide.

Bit flips versus phase flips

Read it aloud"Gamma-phase is about kappa-one times mod-alpha squared. Gamma-bit is proportional to e to the minus two mod-alpha squared. Eta, the bias, is their ratio." Γ is an error rate, in errors per unit time.

6.1 · Derive the phase-flip rate

Single-photon jumps happen at rate \(\kappa_1\langle a^\dagger a\rangle\). Each one does what to the logical qubit? Then predict the decay of \(\langle P\rangle\) starting from \(|C_+\rangle\).
Each jump is a Z error (3.4). A Z error at rate Γ makes \(\langle X\rangle\) decay as \(e^{-2\Gamma t}\).
Step 1. Jumps of \(L_1=\sqrt{\kappa_1}a\) happen at rate \(\kappa_1\langle a^\dagger a\rangle=\kappa_1\bar n\) (from 1.2).
Step 2. Each jump applies \(a\approx\alpha Z\) (3.4), a phase flip. So \(\Gamma_{\rm phase}=\kappa_1\bar n\approx\kappa_1|\alpha|^2\).
Step 3. Jumps are random (Poisson). Probability of \(k\) jumps by time \(t\): \(p_k=e^{-\Gamma t}(\Gamma t)^k/k!\).
Step 4. Each jump flips the sign of parity, so \(\langle P\rangle=\sum_k(-1)^kp_k=e^{-\Gamma t}\sum_k\frac{(-\Gamma t)^k}{k!}=e^{-\Gamma t}e^{-\Gamma t}=e^{-2\Gamma t}\).
Step 5. Result: \(\langle P\rangle(t)=e^{-2\kappa_1\bar nt}\). The rate grows linearly with cat size.

1 · Open systems

Key takeaway

A density matrix plus jump operators describes any leaky quantum system. You get decay rates from one identity.

  • \(\rho\): diagonal = probabilities, off-diagonal = coherences (the "quantum" part). Purity \(\mathrm{Tr}\rho^2=1\) means pure.
  • Lindblad: \(\dot\rho=-i[H,\rho]+\sum_k\big(L_k\rho L_k^\dagger-\tfrac12\{L_k^\dagger L_k,\rho\}\big)\). That is the normal motion plus a "jump" and "no-jump bookkeeping" term per noise source.
  • Jump op = \(\sqrt{\text{rate}}\times\)operator. Loss: \(\sqrt{\kappa}\,a\). Dephasing: \(\sqrt{\kappa_\varphi}\,a^\dagger a\).
  • Averages: \(\frac{d\langle O\rangle}{dt}=i\langle[H,O]\rangle+\sum\langle L^\dagger OL-\tfrac12\{L^\dagger L,O\}\rangle\).
  • Results: loss gives \(\bar n=|\alpha|^2e^{-\kappa t}\) and \(\langle a\rangle=\alpha e^{-i\omega t-\kappa t/2}\) (a spiral). Dephasing keeps \(\bar n\) and erases coherences.
  • Matrices: \(a|n\rangle=\sqrt n|n-1\rangle\), so \(\sqrt n\) sits above the diagonal. \(a^\dagger=a^T\). \(n=\mathrm{diag}(0,1,2,\dots)\).
  • Your screenshot: purity first drops (the state smears), then climbs back as everything decays to the vacuum, which is pure. The grey curve is hidden under ⟨n⟩ because the two agree exactly.
